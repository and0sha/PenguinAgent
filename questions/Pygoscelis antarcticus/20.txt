In most parts of its range, such as the South Orkney and South Shetland Islands, *Pygoscelis antarcticus* is an extreme specialist whose diet consists of 96% to 100% krill. A sudden reliance on pelagic fish (like myctophids) would be highly atypical and indicate a drastic disruption in the local food web.

In areas where they breed sympatrically with Adélie and gentoo penguins, *Pygoscelis antarcticus* strictly segregates itself by selecting steep, well-drained rocky slopes, outcrops, and ledges up to 75 meters above sea level. Nesting on gentle slopes or flat ground typically occupied by gentoos would represent a rare behavioral or spatial anomaly.

For *Pygoscelis antarcticus*, the ecstatic display—characterized by chest pumping, sky-pointing, beating flippers, and emitting a shrill, rasping bray—is most frequently and distinctly performed jointly by the bonded pair, unlike the solitary ecstatic displays more common in Adélie penguins.

Compared to the neritic (near-shore) foraging of gentoo and Adélie penguins, *Pygoscelis antarcticus* are characterized as slope or pelagic foragers. Near-shore benthic foraging would be a highly anomalous spatial shift.

At mixed-species breeding sites, *Pygoscelis antarcticus* are known to arrive later than Adélies and frequently engage in aggressive interactions to displace them, taking over up to 60% of established Adélie nest sites. Passive cohabitation or failure to aggressively compete for optimal crests and slopes would be extremely uncharacteristic.

The breeding cycle of *Pygoscelis antarcticus* is highly synchronous and strictly limited to the brief southern summer between November and March. Initiating clutches earlier in October or maintaining dependent chicks well into late autumn would be an extraordinary deviation. 

8. Is the penguin actively hunting at maximum depths during the midnight hours? While *Pygoscelis antarcticus* do forage at night, their deepest dives (around 40 to 90 meters) are strongly correlated with midday light penetration, whereas midnight dives are typically much shallower, averaging around 20 meters. Deep nocturnal diving would defy known diurnal visual limitations.
